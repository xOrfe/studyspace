# Conjunctions & Linking Words: The Lego Blocks

> [!abstract] **The Concept in a Nutshell**
> Think of **conjunctions** and **linking words** as Lego connectors. Without them, your ideas are just isolated bricks. With them, you can build complex, flowing, and logical arguments. They show addition, cause, contrast, and results!

---

## Scene Study (Real-World Dialogue)
> [!example] **The Launch Delay**
> **Max:** "I wanted to deploy the new features today, **but** the build failed."
> **Chloe:** "**Although** the build failed, the database is safe. **However**, we need to find the bug before we try again."
> **Max:** "Right. **Unless** we fix it tonight, we'll have to delay the launch. Let's work together **so that** we can finish early."

---

## The Blueprint (Form & Structure)

### 1. Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS)
Used to connect words, phrases, or independent clauses of equal grammatical weight.
*   **F**or (cause)
*   **A**nd (addition)
*   **N**or (negative alternative)
*   **B**ut (contrast)
*   **O**r (alternative)
*   **Y**et (contrast/surprise)
*   **S**o (result)
*   *Formula:* `[Clause 1] , + [FANBOYS] + [Clause 2]`
    *   *I wanted to go, **but** it was raining.*

### 2. Subordinating Conjunctions (Clause Connectors)
Introduce a dependent clause that cannot stand alone.
- **Cause:** *because, since, as*
- **Contrast:** *although, even though, while, whereas*
- **Condition:** *if, unless, provided that*
- **Time:** *when, before, after, until, as soon as*
-   *Formula:* `[Subordinating Conjunction] + [Subject] + [Verb] , + [Main Clause]`
    *   ***Although** it was raining, we went out.*

### 3. Transition Adverbs (Sentence Connectors)
Connect independent sentences. They must be preceded by a period or semicolon, and followed by a comma.
- **Contrast:** *however, nevertheless, on the other hand*
- **Result:** *therefore, consequently, as a result*
- **Addition:** *moreover, furthermore, in addition*
-   *Formula:* `[Sentence 1]. [Transition] , [Sentence 2]`
    *   *It was raining. **However**, we decided to go out.*

## Mnemonic & Memory Hacks
> [!tip] **The "Double Bridge" Ban**
> - Never build two bridges for one crossing! In English, you cannot use both **although** and **but** in the same sentence. Choose only one.
>   * ❌ *Although it was raining, but we went out.*
>   * 🔍 *Although it was raining, we went out.* (Correct)
>   * 🔍 *It was raining, but we went out.* (Correct)

## Interactive Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)
- What does "unless" mean? :: "unless" means "if not" (e.g., "Unless you study, you'll fail" = "If you don't study, you'll fail").
- What kind of structure must follow "despite"? :: A noun phrase, pronoun, or gerund (V-ing), NOT a full subject-verb clause.
- Correct this: "He didn't study, however he passed the exam." :: "He didn't study. However, he passed the exam." (or use a semicolon before 'however').

---

## The Trap (Common Mistakes)
> [!danger] **Avoid the Blunder!**
> - **The Mistake:** *"Despite he was sick, he came to work."*
> - **The Fix:** *"Despite **his illness** (or **being sick**), he came to work."*
> - **Why?:** *Despite* is a preposition and must be followed by a noun phrase or gerund. If you want to use a subject-verb clause (*he was sick*), use *although* or *even though*.
